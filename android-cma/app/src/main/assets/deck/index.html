Financial Statement Analysis

Reading the statements: common-size, ratios, EPS, DuPont, and the quality behind the numbers.

20%
A1

Comparative & Common-Size Analysis

Two lenses on the statements: vertical (common-size) compares firms of different sizes at one point in time; horizontal (trend) compares one firm across time.

Vertical — common-size statements

Every line is shown as a % of a base. Income statement & cash-flow lines are a % of net sales/revenue; balance-sheet lines are a % of total assets. This strips out size, so you can compare a $10M firm to a $10B firm — or spot a rising COGS % that signals shrinkage or margin pressure.

Horizontal — trend / common-base-year

Common-base-year %
New line-item amountBase-year line-item amount × 100
Year-over-year growth %
New amount − Old amountOld amount
Base-year vs YoY — don't confuse themCommon-base-year % restates every year as a proportion of the first year (base = 100%). It is not a period-to-period increase. YoY growth compares each year to the immediately prior year.

The income-statement ladder (EBIT vs EBT vs operating income)

LevelWhat it includes / excludes
Operating incomeCore business only. Excludes interest & dividend income, interest expense, non-operating gains/losses, discontinued ops.
Income from continuing opsOperating income + financial income/expense + non-operating items. Excludes discontinued ops.
EBITOperating income + interest & dividend income + non-operating gains/losses + pre-tax discontinued-op results.
EBTEBIT − interest expense.
Exam shortcutIf a firm has no interest/dividend income, no non-operating gains/losses, and no discontinued ops, then operating income = EBIT. Exams often let you use them interchangeably under that assumption — but know they are conceptually different.
A2

Liquidity Ratios

Can the firm pay its short-term bills as they come due? Five ratios, each stricter than the last on what counts as "liquid."

Current ratio
Current assetsCurrent liabilities  benchmark ≈ 2:1
Quick / acid-test ratio
Cash + cash equiv. + current marketable securities + net A/RCurrent liabilities
Cash ratio
Cash + cash equivalents + current marketable securitiesCurrent liabilities
Cash-flow ratio

Operating cash flow ÷ period-end CL

Annualize the numerator. Healthy ≈ 0.40+. Uses period-end CL (more conservative).

Net working capital ratio

(CA − CL) ÷ Total assets

Negative = current liabilities exceed current assets — a serious warning sign.

What's excluded from the quick ratio — and whyInventory is out (sold inventory must be replaced → needs cash; also less liquid than A/R). Prepaid expenses are out (they can't be converted to cash — they only avoid a future outflow).
Operating cycle drives the "right" current ratioShorter operating cycle → cash comes in faster → the firm can run safely on a lower current ratio. Longer cycle → needs a higher ratio. Quality of receivables/inventory matters more than the raw number.
A3

Leverage, Solvency & Coverage

Solvency is paying long-term obligations from earnings. Two ideas: capital-structure ratios (how much debt) and coverage ratios (can earnings service it).

The three leverage measures (DOL, DFL, DTL)

Degree of operating leverage (fixed operating costs)
% Δ EBIT% Δ Sales = Contribution marginEBIT
Degree of financial leverage (fixed interest)
% Δ Net income% Δ EBIT = EBITEBT
Degree of total leverage
DTL = DOL × DFL = % Δ Net income% Δ Sales = Contribution marginEBT
Ratio-form uses the earlier year onlyCM÷EBIT, EBIT÷EBT and CM÷EBT give the DOL/DFL/DTL for the earlier of two periods. Leverage isn't static — as sales rise (CM ratio & fixed costs held constant), all three degrees decrease.

Capital-structure ratios

Debt-to-equity
Total liabilities ÷ Total equity

Screening ratio. >2:1 → dig deeper into other ratios.

Long-term D/E
(Total debt − Current liab.) ÷ Total equity

Noncurrent LT debt only. >1:1 = more debt- than equity-financed.

Debt to total assets
Total liabilities ÷ Total assets

Includes all liabilities (even A/P) → conservative.

Financial leverage / equity multiplier
Total assets ÷ Total equity

Higher = more debt. "Trading on the equity."

"Trading on the equity"Borrowing to invest. Successful when return on assets > after-tax cost of debt (common shareholders benefit). Unsuccessful when ROA < after-tax cost of debt — leverage magnifies the loss.

Earnings-coverage ratios

Interest coverage (times interest earned)
EBIT ÷ Interest expense  >3.0 excellent · <1.5 danger
Fixed charge coverage
EBIT + operating/short-term lease paymentsInterest + required principal + all lease payments
Cash flow to fixed charges
Adj. operating CF (add back cash interest, leases, cash taxes)Interest + required principal + all lease payments
Principal payments live in the denominator, not the add-backFor cash-flow-to-fixed-charges, add back cash interest, lease payments and cash taxes (they reduced operating CF). Do not add back principal repayments — they're financing activities, so they never hit operating CF.
A4

Activity Ratios & the Cash Cycle

How efficiently does the firm turn over receivables, inventory, and payables? Turnover ratios and their "days" companions.

Receivables
A/R turnover = Net credit sales ÷ Avg gross A/R Days' sales in A/R = 365 ÷ A/R turnover

↑ turnover = collecting faster.

Inventory
Inv turnover = COGS ÷ Avg inventory Days' sales in inv = 365 ÷ Inv turnover

Too high can mean stock-outs.

Payables
A/P turnover = Credit purchases ÷ Avg A/P Days' purch. in A/P = 365 ÷ A/P turnover

↓ turnover = paying slower (liquidity risk).

Asset turnover
Total = Sales ÷ Avg total assets Fixed = Sales ÷ Avg net PP&E

Revenue generated per $ of assets.

Operating cycle & cash conversion cycle
Operating cycle = Days' sales in A/R + Days' sales in inventory
Cash cycle = Operating cycle − Days' purchases in A/P
Negative cash cycle = excellentIf a firm collects (or gets paid up-front) before it must pay suppliers, its cash cycle can go negative — it's financing operations with suppliers' money. Amazon-style. A small reduction in the cash cycle can lift pre-tax profit materially at scale.
Gross A/R, not netA/R turnover uses average gross receivables (before the allowance for credit losses) to match the numerator, which is net of returns/allowances but includes sales to customers who won't pay.
A5

Earnings Per Share — Basic & Diluted

BEPS is earnings for shares actually outstanding. DEPS assumes every dilutive convertible/option was exercised on day one.

Basic EPS
Net income − Preferred dividends (IAC)Weighted-avg common shares outstanding (WANCSO)
Which preferred dividend do you subtract?Cumulative preferred: subtract the dividend earned this year, declared or not. Noncumulative: subtract only if declared. (If the exam is silent on a preferred dividend, assume it was declared.)

WANCSO — weighting the shares

EventHow it's weighted
New issue / buybackWeighted only for the portion of the year the shares were outstanding.
Stock split / stock dividendApplied retroactively to Jan 1 (and all prior comparative periods). Adjust every balance & transaction that occurred before the split/dividend.
Treasury stockOnly subtract if reacquired during the year; already-held treasury needs no adjustment.
Split/dividend timing trapShares issued or bought after a split/stock dividend are not adjusted for it. Only pre-split/pre-dividend balances get multiplied up.

Diluted EPS — the two methods

Options / warrants → treasury-stock method
  • Assume exercised at start; proceeds buy back shares at the average market price.
  • Dilutive only if exercise price < average market price.
  • Adds shares only (no income effect).
Convertibles → if-converted method
  • Bond effect = interest × (1 − tax) ÷ shares
  • Preferred effect = dividend ÷ shares
  • Add both to IAC and WANCSO.
Rank most-to-least dilutive; stop at antidilutiveCompute each security's EPS effect, add them lowest effect first. Once adding a security would raise intermediate EPS, it's antidilutive — exclude it and every one after it.
A6

Market Ratios

Per-share metrics that only mean anything for publicly traded firms, because they use the secondary-market share price.

Book value per share
(Total equity − Preferred) ÷ Common shares outstanding

Uses period-end shares, not WANCSO.

Market-to-book
Market price/share ÷ Book value/share

>1 usually — appreciated assets aren't written up under GAAP.

Price / earnings
Market price/share ÷ Basic EPS

High = market expects growth. Meaningless if EPS < 0.

Earnings yield
Basic EPS ÷ Market price/share

The inverse of P/E.

Dividend yield
Annual dividend/share ÷ Market price/share
Dividend payout
Dividend/share ÷ Basic EPS  (or total common div ÷ IAC)
Shareholder return
[(End price − Begin price) + Annual dividend/share] ÷ Begin price

Capital gain + dividend, as a % of the beginning price.

Book value ≠ market value ≠ fair valueBook value is just accumulated accounting entries. Fixed assets sit at historical cost less depreciation; appreciated and internally-generated intangibles aren't on the books — so market value normally exceeds book.
A7

Profitability & DuPont Analysis

Margins measure profit per dollar of sales; DuPont decomposes returns to show why ROA and ROE move.

Margins (all ÷ net sales)
Gross = Gross profit ÷ Sales Operating = Operating income ÷ Sales Net = Net income ÷ Sales EBITDA = EBITDA ÷ Sales
Returns on capital
ROA = Net income ÷ Avg total assets ROE = Net income ÷ Avg total equity ROCE = (NI − Pref div) ÷ Avg common equity
DuPont decomposition
ROA = Net profit margin × Total asset turnover
ROE = Net margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier
Three levers of ROE(1) operating efficiency = net margin, (2) asset-use efficiency = total asset turnover, (3) leverage = equity multiplier (avg assets ÷ avg equity). Improve any one and ROE rises.
Sustainable growth rate
(1 − Dividend payout ratio) × Return on common equity
RNOA — an operating-only variantReturn on net operating assets = NOPAT ÷ net operating assets, stripping out financing (interest expense, investment income) to isolate operating performance.
Older assets flatter ROA/ROEMore accumulated depreciation → lower asset book value (denominator) and lower depreciation expense → higher net income (numerator). A firm with old assets can post a higher ROA for the same operations.
A8

Special Issues — FX, Changes & Earnings Quality

The judgment-heavy corner of Section A: foreign-currency conversion, accounting changes, and how "real" the earnings are.

Remeasurement vs Translation

Remeasurement (temporal)Translation (current-rate)
PurposeCurrency of record → functionalFunctional → reporting (USD)
Monetary itemsCurrent rateCurrent rate
Nonmonetary (fixed assets, inventory)Historical rateCurrent rate
Gain/loss goes toIncome (continuing ops)OCI (equity)
Functional currency drives the pathIf currency of record = functional currency → only translate (one step). If functional = USD → remeasuring also converts to USD (one step). If all three differ → remeasure then translate (two steps).

Accounting changes & corrections

Retrospective application

Change in principle (e.g. FIFO→LIFO) & change in reporting entity. Restate prior periods "as adjusted."

Prospective adjustment

Change in estimate — and change in depreciation method (= estimate change effected by principle change). Current & future only.

Restatement

Correction of an error (incl. non-GAAP → GAAP). Label prior periods "as restated."

Economic vs accounting profit

Economic profit subtracts implicit/opportunity costs too, so it's never higher than accounting profit.

Earnings quality & persistence

Conservative accounting = higher-quality earningsIn rising prices, LIFO and accelerated depreciation are conservative (lower current income, higher quality). FIFO + straight-line are least conservative. Watch for "big bath," income smoothing, and channel-stuffing (rising A/R vs sales).

B1

Risk & Return — CAPM and Beta

Two risk families: systematic (market-wide, undiversifiable) and unsystematic (firm-specific, diversifiable). CAPM prices only the systematic part.

Capital Asset Pricing Model
R = RF + β(RM − RF)   where (RM − RF) = market risk premium
Risk premium of the stock = β × market risk premiumThe stock's own risk premium is β(RM − RF). Rearranged: β = (R − RF) ÷ (RM − RF). RF ≈ short-term U.S. Treasury bills.

Reading beta

BetaMeaning
= 1.0Moves with the market (market portfolio β = 1).
> 1.0More volatile than market — "aggressive."
0 – 1.0Less volatile — "defensive."
= 0Risk-free; no correlation with market.
< 0Moves opposite the market.
Systematic (market) risk
  • Inflation, recession, war, macro shocks.
  • Sub-types: market, interest-rate, purchasing-power, FX risk.
  • Cannot be diversified away.
Unsystematic (company) risk
  • Credit/default, liquidity, business risk.
  • Specific to a firm or industry.
  • Can be reduced by diversification.
Diversification & correlationZero portfolio risk requires two perfectly negatively correlated assets. Low-beta, diversified holdings minimize risk. The SML plots CAPM: intercept = RF, slope = market risk premium; a stock above the SML is undervalued.
B2

Bond Valuation

A bond's price is the PV of its coupons (an annuity) plus the PV of its face value (a lump sum), discounted at the market rate.

Bond price
Price = (Coupon × PVIFA) + (Face × PVIF)
Semi-annual: halve coupon & rate, double the periods.
The premium/discount rule — memorize the directionCoupon rate > market rate → sells at a premium (price > face). Coupon < market → discount. Coupon = market → at par. Rates up → existing bond prices (and stock prices) fall.

Bond types & features

By security
  • Secured — backed by collateral (least risky).
  • Debenture — backed only by full faith/credit.
  • Income bond — pays interest only if earned.
  • Junk — below investment grade, high yield.
Features that cut the coupon
  • Conversion option, sinking fund, higher rating (AA→AAA) → lower coupon.
  • Call provision → raises the coupon (bad for investor).
  • Serial bonds — staggered maturities investors can pick.
Call vs put & the yield curveA call provision lets the issuer redeem early (compensated by a higher coupon). The yield curve plots yield (y-axis) vs maturity (x-axis) — the term structure of interest rates.
B3

Stock Valuation & Dividend Policy

The dividend-discount / Gordon growth model values a share as the PV of a growing dividend stream.

Gordon (constant) growth model
P0 = D1k − g   D1 = next dividend, k = required return, g = growth

Dividend payout process — four dates

DateWhat happens
DeclarationBoard votes; records dividends payable (liability).
Ex-dividendBuy on/after this date → no dividend (2 days before record).
RecordDetermines who's eligible.
PaymentCash paid; Dr dividends payable, Cr cash.
Dividend policies
  • Residual — reinvest first, pay out whatever's left.
  • Active/stable — prioritize a steady dividend.
Dividend types
  • Cash, property (in-kind), stock (new shares).
  • Liquidating — exceeds retained earnings; not taxable.
Treasury stock isn't an assetRepurchased shares are a reduction of equity, not an asset. Preferred dividends are discretionary — skipping them is not a default.
B4

Cost of Capital & WACC

WACC is the market-value-weighted, after-tax cost of every financing source — the hurdle rate for an average-risk project.

Cost of debt
After-tax = Before-tax × (1 − tax rate)

Before-tax = coupon if at par; else YTM/market rate; else current yield.

Cost of preferred
D ÷ (V − F)

F = flotation cost; no tax adjustment (dividends aren't deductible).

Cost of new common
D₁ ÷ (V − F) + g
Cost of retained earnings
D₁ ÷ V + g

No flotation cost → cheaper than new equity.

WACC & the marginal cost break point
WACC = Σ (weight × after-tax cost), weights at market value
Break point = Retained earnings availableWeight of equity in structure
Retained earnings are not freeThey carry an opportunity cost equal to the cost of common equity (minus flotation). Beyond the break point the firm must issue new (costlier) equity, so WACC steps up. Weight by market value, never book value.
CAPM gives cost of equityYou can also estimate the cost of equity directly with CAPM: RF + β(RM − RF). Cost of capital = the minimum return so shareholders' interests aren't diluted.
B5

Derivatives & Hedging

A hedge is an offsetting position so a loss on one side is cancelled by a gain on the other. Four instrument families.

InstrumentKey feature
ForwardCustomized, OTC, both parties obligated. Counterparty default risk.
FutureStandardized, exchange-traded; exchange guarantees → no counterparty risk.
OptionRight, not obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put).
SwapPrivate agreement to exchange future cash flows (e.g. interest-rate swap).

Options — moneyness & value

Call option (right to buy)
  • In-the-money when Strike < Spot.
  • Buy to protect against a rising price / FX payable.
Put option (right to sell)
  • In-the-money when Strike > Spot.
  • Buy to protect against a falling price / FX receivable.
Option premium
Premium = Intrinsic value + Extrinsic (time) value
American vs European · covered vs nakedAmerican = exercisable any time; European = only at maturity. A covered call means the writer owns the stock; a naked call (no underlying) is far riskier for the writer. Out-of-the-money options have zero intrinsic value.
B6

Working Capital Management

Managing current assets and liabilities so the firm meets short-term obligations — trading off liquidity against profitability.

Net working capital
Current assets − Current liabilities
The core trade-offMore liquidity → more invested in low-return current assets → lower profitability. Less liquidity → higher returns but more risk. That's the inverse relationship between liquidity and profitability.
Conservative policy
  • High current assets, high current ratio.
  • Low liquidity risk, lower returns.
Aggressive policy
  • Lean current assets, lower current ratio.
  • Higher returns, higher liquidity risk.

Key working-capital calculations

EOQ
√(2aD ÷ k)

a=order cost, D=annual demand, k=carrying cost/unit.

Cost of forgoing a discount
(disc% ÷ (100−disc%)) × (365 ÷ (pay period − disc period))
Permanent vs temporary WC

Permanent = minimum maintained all year; temporary = seasonal increases.

Float

Speed up collections (reduce collection float); slow disbursements — legitimately.

Cash motivesFirms hold cash for three reasons: transactions, precautionary, and speculative. JIT inventory drives holding costs toward zero but raises stock-out risk.
B7

Raising Capital & Restructuring

Growing (or shrinking) through mergers, acquisitions, divestitures, and buyouts — plus how targets are valued and defended.

M&A classifications

By relationship
  • Horizontal — same line of business.
  • Vertical — different production stages (forward = toward consumer; backward = toward raw materials).
  • Conglomerate — unrelated businesses.
Why combine
  • Economies of scale — share centralized services (beware dis-economies).
  • Synergy — combined value > sum of the parts.

Takeover defenses

Pre-offer
  • Staggered board, supermajority approval.
  • Golden parachute, poison pill, poison put.
Post-offer
  • White knight, Pac-Man (reverse tender).
  • Lockup, leveraged recapitalization, issue new stock.

Divestitures & valuation

Free cash flow (for DCF valuation)
FCF = EBIT(1 − t) + noncash exp. − Δ working capital − capex
Divestiture toolkitSpin-off — shares of a new independent firm given to existing holders. Equity carve-out — IPO of a subsidiary for cash (parent keeps majority). LBO/MBO — buy with mostly debt, collateralized by the target's own assets.
B8

International Finance

Exchange-rate mechanics, the parity relationships, and the tools to hedge cross-border cash flows and settle international trade.

Quotes, appreciation & cross rates

Base currency = 1 unitIn EUR/USD = 1.36, the euro (base) = 1 and costs 1.36 dollars. A currency appreciates when one of its units buys more of the other. Appreciation of A vs B always means depreciation of B vs A.
Cross rate & forward rate
Cross rate (A per B) = (USD per B) ÷ (USD per A)
Forward: F = S × (1 + idomestic) ÷ (1 + iforeign)

Parity & forward premium/discount

RelationshipResult
Foreign rate > domestic rateForward foreign currency at a discount (expected to depreciate).
Foreign rate < domestic rateForward foreign currency at a premium.
PPPExchange rate = foreign price level ÷ domestic price level.
Currency appreciatesImports ↑, exports ↓, trade balance → negative.
Currency depreciatesImports ↓, exports ↑, trade balance → positive.
Hedging exchange-rate risk
  • Natural hedge — same-currency revenues & costs.
  • Operational — balance FX payables & receivables.
  • Forwards, futures, swaps, options.
Trade-finance tools
  • Prepayment, sight draft, letter of credit.
  • Cross-border factoring, forfaiting (>$500k).
  • Countertrade / barter; banker's acceptance.
Spot vs forward directionWhen the spot rate is higher than the forward rate (price of 1 foreign unit), the foreign currency sells at a forward discount. Convert both quotes to "price of 1 foreign unit" before comparing.
B9

Cash & Marketable Securities Management

Cash earns nothing, so the goal is to hold the minimum consistent with paying bills — speeding collections, slowing disbursements, and parking the surplus safely.

Why firms hold cash

MotivePurpose
TransactionsFund ordinary day-to-day payments.
PrecautionaryBuffer against unexpected needs.
SpeculativeBe ready to seize bargains or opportunities.

Float and the collection/disbursement toolkit

Speed up inflows
  • Lock-box — customers pay to a bank-run PO box.
  • Concentration banking; electronic funds transfer.
  • Offer cash discounts to accelerate payment.
Slow down outflows
  • Zero-balance accounts (ZBA) — funded only as cheques clear.
  • Controlled disbursement accounts.
  • Centralize payables; pay on (not before) the due date.
Lock-box is a collection toolA classic exam trap lists the lock-box among "disbursement" techniques. It manages inflows. A ZBA reduces idle balances, cost, and admin time — but it preserves disbursement float rather than reducing it.
Baumol optimal cash balance
Q = 2 × annual disbursements × cost per transactionopportunity interest rate (square root of)
Read the formula's leversHigher transaction cost or annual disbursements (numerator) → hold more cash. A higher return on marketable securities (denominator) raises the opportunity cost of idle cash → hold less.

Where to park surplus cash

Safety and liquidity beat yieldSuitable: Treasury bills (sold at a discount, no coupon, no default risk; interest is taxable federally but exempt from state/local tax), commercial paper, money-market funds, negotiable CDs, repos. Unsuitable: municipal bonds (long-dated, and their tax-exempt yield is wasted on a corporation) and equities.
B10

Short-Term Financing & Effective Rates

The stated rate is rarely the real one. Anything that reduces the cash you actually get to use — discounted interest, a compensating balance — raises the effective rate.

The master pattern
Effective rate = Interest paidUsable funds
Simple interest
Effective = stated rate

Interest paid at maturity; full principal usable.

Discounted note
r ÷ (1 − r)

Interest deducted up front, so you receive less than face.

Compensating balance
r ÷ (1 − c)

A fraction c must stay on deposit, shrinking usable funds.

Both together
r ÷ (1 − r − c)

Discounted interest and a compensating balance.

Face value needed to net a required amount (discounted note)
Face = Funds needed1 − stated rate

Trade credit — the cost of forgoing a discount

Annualized cost of not taking the discount
Discount %100 − Discount % × 360Days paid − Discount period
Compare it to your borrowing rateTerms of 2/10, net 45 cost (2÷98) × (360÷35) ≈ 21% annually. If you can borrow at 12%, borrow and take the discount. If payment slips past the due date, the credit period lengthens and the annualized cost falls.
Other short-term sourcesCommercial paper (large, high-credit issuers only), lines of credit & revolving agreements, factoring or pledging receivables, and inventory-secured loans (floating lien, trust receipt, warehouse receipt).
Watch the denominatorEvery one of these questions turns on usable funds. Discounted interest subtracts r; a compensating balance subtracts c; both subtract both. Get the denominator right and the arithmetic is trivial.

C1

CVP & Breakeven Analysis

Contribution margin = the slice of each sales dollar left after variable costs, available to cover fixed costs and then profit.

Contribution margin
UCM = Price − Variable cost/unitCM ratio = UCM ÷ Price
Breakeven
Units = Fixed costs ÷ UCMRevenue = Fixed costs ÷ CM ratio

Target profit

Target dollar profit — treat as extra fixed cost
Units = Fixed costs + Target pre-tax profitUnit contribution margin
Target profit as % of sales — treat as extra variable cost
Units = Fixed costsPrice − VC/unit − (Target % × Price)
The two treatments — and the tax conversionA target dollar profit acts like an added fixed cost (numerator). A target %-of-sales profit acts like an added variable cost (shrinks the denominator). Convert after-tax to pre-tax first: Pre-tax = After-tax ÷ (1 − tax rate).

CVP assumptions

The model's fine printAll costs are strictly fixed or variable (no mixed); linear revenue & cost within the relevant range; constant price, variable cost/unit, fixed cost, and sales mix; production = sales (no inventory change); time value of money ignored. Reasonable in the short run.
Decision usesBreakeven logic answers "how many extra units justify this marketing spend?" (add the new fixed cost to the numerator) and "will a price cut increase profit?" (rework UCM at the lower price).
C2

Marginal Analysis & Relevant Costs

Short-run decisions turn on relevant costs only — future costs that differ between alternatives. Ignore sunk and unavoidable costs.

Relevant (include)Irrelevant (exclude)
Incremental revenue & variable costsSunk (past) costs
Avoidable fixed costsCommitted / unavoidable costs
Opportunity costsCommon & allocated costs

Make-or-buy

Maximum price to pay an outside supplier
Total internal cost − Unavoidable costs = Avoidable cost (the ceiling)
Compare avoidable cost to the buy priceBuy only if the outside price is less than the avoidable cost of making it. If freed-up capacity can be rented or used elsewhere, add that opportunity benefit to the case for buying.

Special orders & shut-down

Special order
  • If idle capacity: accept when price > incremental (usually variable) cost.
  • Fixed costs usually irrelevant unless the order adds them.
Shut-down price
  • Price just covers average variable cost.
  • Below it → shut down (can't even cover variable cost).
Income taxes only matter when they differTaxes are relevant to a decision only if they change between the alternatives (e.g. location or transfer-pricing decisions). Depreciation itself is never a relevant cash flow — but its tax shield is.
C3

Pricing & Market Structures

Price elasticity and market structure govern how much pricing power a firm has and how a price change hits total revenue.

Price elasticity of demand

Midpoint (arc) method
(Q₂ − Q₁) ÷ [(Q₂ + Q₁)/2](P₂ − P₁) ÷ [(P₂ + P₁)/2]
ElasticityA price increase means…
Elastic (E > 1)Total revenue falls (quantity drops more than price rises).
Inelastic (E < 1)Total revenue rises.
Unitary (E = 1)Total revenue unchanged.
Elastic → cut price to grow revenueIf demand is elastic, a price decrease raises total revenue. With E = 2.5, a 10% price cut lifts quantity ~25%. Inverse relation between price and quantity (law of demand).

The four market structures

StructurePricing behaviour
Perfect competitionPrice taker — sells at market price; MR = price.
MonopolyPrice maker; downward demand curve; MR < price. Produce where MR = MC.
Monopolistic competitionMany firms, differentiated products; some pricing power; economic profit → 0 long run.
OligopolyFew firms; kinked demand curve; sticky prices. Rivals match cuts, ignore hikes.
All structures produce where MR = MCEvery firm maximizes profit at MR = MC; what differs is the price they can charge for that quantity. Cost-plus and target costing (target cost = market price − desired profit) are common pricing methods.
C4

Supply, Demand & Elasticity

Price is set where supply meets demand. Exam questions test whether you can tell a shift of the curve from a movement along it.

The single most-tested distinctionA change in the good's own price causes a movement along the curve. Anything else shifts the whole curve.
Demand shifts right (increases) when…Demand shifts left (decreases) when…
Consumer income rises (normal good)Income rises for an inferior good
Price of a substitute risesPrice of a substitute falls
Price of a complement fallsPrice of a complement rises
Population/tastes grow favourablyTastes shift away; population falls

Equilibrium, shortage and surplus

Above and below the clearing pricePrice above equilibrium → quantity supplied exceeds demanded → surplus (excess supply). Price below equilibrium → shortage (excess demand). If production costs rise, supply shifts left and the clearing price rises; if costs fall, price falls.

Elasticity and total revenue

Marginal revenue when price must fall to sell more
MR = TRn − TRn−1 — below price, since all units are repriced
Elastic (E > 1)
  • %ΔQ > %ΔP.
  • Price ↑ → revenue falls.
  • Price ↓ → revenue rises.
Inelastic (E < 1)
  • %ΔQ < %ΔP.
  • Price ↑ → revenue rises.
  • Price ↓ → revenue falls.
What makes demand elasticClose substitutes available, the item is a large share of income, it is a luxury rather than a necessity, and the buyer has a long time to adjust. Necessities with few substitutes are inelastic.
C5

Pricing Methods & the Product Life Cycle

Two families: build the price up from cost, or work backwards from what the market will pay.

MethodHow it works & when it fits
Cost-based (cost-plus)Product cost + markup. Fits made-to-order, custom, or one-off work where no market price exists. Promotes price stability and full cost recovery, but allocating fixed cost to units is arbitrary.
Market-based (target costing)Start from the price the market will bear, subtract required profit → the allowable cost. Customer-driven, external focus; used where competition is stiff.
Life-cycle pricingPrice must recover costs across the whole life cycle — R&D and design through production, marketing, and disposal — not just manufacturing.
Price discriminationDifferent prices to different customers for the same good, where markets can be separated and resale prevented.
Target cost
Target cost = Market price − Required profit
Value engineering closes the gapWhen the projected cost exceeds the target cost, value engineering examines each function and feature — trading off features against cost at the design stage — to remove cost without losing customer-valued function. (Kaizen is continuous improvement during production; ABC is a costing method, not a cost-reduction technique.)

Launch strategies

Price skimming
  • Set price high at launch.
  • Harvest early adopters; recover R&D fast.
  • Needs a differentiated or novel product.
Penetration pricing
  • Set price low at launch.
  • Buy market share and deter entrants.
  • Suits elastic demand and scale economies.
Later in the cycleAs the product matures and rivals copy it, price is defended by differentiating — adding features, service, or branding — rather than by cost recovery alone.

Risk Management

Identify, quantify, and respond to enterprise risk — anchored by the COSO 2017 ERM framework.

10%
D1

Risk Types & Terminology

A risk is any event that could stop the organization achieving its objectives. Know the categories and the "inherent vs residual" distinction.

Risk typeSource
BusinessVariability in earnings from normal operations (demand, price, cost, operating leverage).
StrategicHigh-level decisions / long-term planning; incl. political risk.
OperationalInternal process/system/people failures; tied to fixed-cost proportion.
Legal & complianceLawsuits; violations of laws, rules, internal policies.
FinancialInability to finance — liquidity (short-term) & solvency (long-term); FX, rates, commodities.
HazardInsurable events — natural disasters, key-person death, injury.
Inherent vs residual risk
Inherent risk − Mitigation actions = Residual risk
Volatility and time both raise riskMore volatility (inconsistent results) → more uncertainty → more risk. A longer time horizon is riskier than a shorter one. Systematic = market/undiversifiable; unsystematic = specific/diversifiable.
D2

Risk Assessment & Quantification

Assess each risk on two axes — frequency (likelihood) and severity (impact) — then quantify where possible.

Expected loss (weighted average)
Expected loss = Σ (probability × loss amount)

Quantitative tools

Value at Risk (VaR)

Max loss over a period at a confidence level. "$100M at 1-week 95%" → only 5% chance of losing more. Not for illiquid assets. Methods: historical, variance-covariance, Monte Carlo.

CFaR / EaR

Cash-Flow at Risk — worst cash-flow shortfall at a confidence level. Earnings at Risk — worst earnings change (e.g. from a rate move).

Four loss measures
  • Expected loss (budget it).
  • Unexpected loss (reserve capital).
  • Maximum probable loss (PML).
  • Maximum possible / catastrophic loss.
Risk map (heat map)

Plots likelihood (x) vs impact (y), 1–8 scale each. Can show inherent or residual risk; gives a portfolio view.

Cost-benefit gate for mitigationRespond only when the expected cost of the risk exceeds the cost of the response. Qualitative tools (risk maps, ranking most-to-least) matter because qualitative risks lead to quantitative losses.
D3

Risk Response & COSO ERM

Four responses, chosen by the probability × impact grid — then the whole thing sits inside the COSO 2017 framework.

Response matrix (probability × impact)
High/High → Avoid  ·  High/Low → Reduce (controls)
Low/High → Transfer (insurance/hedge)  ·  Low/Low → Accept/Retain
Appetite vs toleranceRisk appetite = the broad level of risk the firm will accept pursuing value. Risk tolerance = the narrower acceptable variation around a specific objective. Retained risk = the uninsured portion (e.g. a deductible / self-insurance).

The 5-step risk process

Identify → Assess → Prioritize → Respond → MonitorTechniques for identification: brainstorming, event inventories & loss data, interviews/self-assessment, facilitated workshops, SWOT, questionnaires/surveys, scenario ("what-if") analysis.

COSO 2017 ERM — 5 components, 20 principles

ComponentFocus
Governance & CultureBoard oversight; sets the tone.
Strategy & Objective-SettingAlign risk appetite with strategy.
PerformanceIdentify, assess severity, prioritize, respond, portfolio view.
Review & RevisionIterative — reassess as conditions change.
Information, Communication & ReportingReport risk, culture, performance.
Capital adequacy ratio (banks)
CAR = Tier 1 + Tier 2 capitalRisk-weighted assets  minimum 8%
Tier 1 vs Tier 2Tier 1 (core) absorbs losses while operating — common stock, retained earnings, perpetual non-cumulative preferred. Tier 2 (supplementary) only absorbs losses in wind-up. Riskier assets raise RWA, which lowers the CAR.

Investment Decisions

Building the incremental cash-flow stream and ranking projects with NPV, IRR, PI, and payback.

10%
E1

Estimating Project Cash Flows

Capital budgeting uses only incremental, after-tax cash flows — future flows that differ because of the project.

RelevantIrrelevant
Incremental revenue & variable costsSunk costs (already incurred)
Opportunity costCommitted & allocated costs
Capex & incremental working capitalInterest on debt, dividends*
Salvage / residual valueCommon & unavoidable costs

*Financing flows are captured in the discount rate, not the cash-flow stream.

Depreciation tax shield
Tax shield = Depreciation × Tax rate
Three cash-flow buckets(1) Initial — asset cost + install + initial working capital. (2) Interim/operating — after-tax operating CF + depreciation tax shield ± ΔWC. (3) Terminal — after-tax salvage + release of working capital.
MACRS & straight-line for taxTax depreciation (MACRS, half-year convention) is used in capital budgeting, and the depreciable base is 100% of cost — do not subtract salvage for tax purposes, even though GAAP would.
E2

Capital Budgeting Techniques

Two families: traditional (ignore time value) and discounted-cash-flow (respect it).

Traditional
Payback = Investment ÷ annual CF (even) ARR = Avg net income ÷ investment

Payback ignores TVM & flows after cutoff. ARR uses profit, not cash.

Discounted
NPV = PV inflows − investment PI = PV inflows ÷ investment IRR: the rate where NPV = 0
IRR by interpolation
IRR = LR + NPV at LRNPV at LR − NPV at HR × (HR − LR)
Accept rulesNPV ≥ 0 · PI ≥ 1 (or ≥ 0 if PI uses NPV in numerator) · IRR ≥ cost of capital · payback ≤ target. NPV gives an absolute dollar figure and maximizes shareholder wealth.
NPV vs IRR conflict → NPV winsFor mutually exclusive projects the two can disagree; always follow NPV. IRR pitfalls: assumes reinvestment at the IRR, gives only a %, and non-conventional cash flows can produce multiple IRRs.
Annuity IRR shortcutIf cash flows are level, Investment ÷ annual CF = the annuity factor; find that factor in the row for the project's life — the column heading is the IRR.
E3

Risk Analysis & Real Options

Because forecasts are uncertain, projects are stress-tested — and the flexibility to change course itself has value.

Handling project risk
  • Sensitivity — flex one variable at a time.
  • Scenario — best/base/worst combinations.
  • Simulation (Monte Carlo) — random trials → distribution.
  • Risk-adjusted discount rate / certainty equivalents.
Real options (managerial flexibility)
  • Option to expand, abandon, delay/wait, or switch inputs/outputs.
  • Ignoring them understates a project's value.
Longer & more volatile = riskierA longer horizon and greater cash-flow volatility both increase risk, so they call for a higher discount rate. Discounted payback adds TVM to plain payback but still ignores flows after the cutoff.

F1

Business Ethics & Fraud

Ethics = the moral principles, values, and norms that guide behaviour. Business ethics applies them to commercial conduct, going beyond the letter of the law.

Foundational concepts

TermMeaning
Conflict of interestPersonal interest interferes with duty to the employer/client.
Fiduciary dutyDuty to act in another party's best interest (loyalty + care).
Due diligenceReasonable care/investigation before a decision.
WhistleblowerReports illegal/unethical acts; protected by law (e.g. Dodd-Frank, SOX).
Bribe vs facilitation paymentBribe = improper inducement; facilitation = small payment to speed a routine act.

Three views of fairness

Fairness principles
  • Equality — treat like cases alike.
  • Reciprocity — fair exchange / mutual benefit.
  • Optimization — maximize total welfare efficiently.
Companion virtues
  • Integrity — consistency of actions and values.
  • Objectivity — free from bias/conflict.
  • Transparency — open, honest disclosure.

Types of fraud

CategoryExamples
Asset misappropriationTheft, skimming, payroll/expense fraud — most common, lowest $.
Financial-statement fraudManipulating results — least common, highest $.
CorruptionBribery, kickbacks, conflicts of interest.
Marketing fraudPuffery, implied falsity, literal falsity, ambiguous falsity.
Consumer fraudFriendly fraud, wardrobing, price arbitrage.
The inverse frequency/cost ruleAsset misappropriation is the most frequent but least costly per scheme; financial-statement fraud is the least frequent but most costly. Corruption sits in between.
F2

IMA Statement & the Fraud Triangle

The IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice is the single most heavily tested item in this section. Memorize the 4 overarching principles and the 4 standards.

4 Overarching Principles

Honesty · Fairness · Objectivity · ResponsibilityThese are the values members are expected to uphold; failure to comply may result in disciplinary action.

4 Standards

StandardCore requirement
CompetenceMaintain skills; perform duties per laws/standards; provide decision-support that is accurate, clear, concise, timely; recognize and communicate professional limitations.
ConfidentialityKeep information confidential except when disclosure is authorized or legally required; don't use it for unethical/illegal advantage.
IntegrityMitigate conflicts of interest; abstain from activity that discredits the profession; avoid conduct that prejudices carrying out duties ethically.
CredibilityCommunicate fairly and objectively; disclose all relevant information; disclose deficiencies in internal controls/info.
MnemonicStandards → "C-C-I-C" (Competence, Confidentiality, Integrity, Credibility). Principles → "HFOR" (Honesty, Fairness, Objectivity, Responsibility).

Resolving an ethical conflict

Escalation path(1) Follow the organization's own policy. (2) If unresolved, escalate up the chain — immediate supervisor first; if the supervisor is involved, go to the next level (audit committee/board). (3) Consult your own attorney / the IMA Ethics Helpline. Do not go straight to outside authorities or resign as a first step; contacting authorities generally isn't considered appropriate unless legally required.

The fraud triangle

Three conditions present in most fraud
Pressure/Incentive  +  Opportunity  +  Rationalization
Cut any one legInternal controls attack opportunity (the leg management most controls). Remove any single element and the fraud is far less likely. Some frameworks add a fourth — capability (the "fraud diamond").
F3

Organizational Ethics, CSR & Data Ethics

Ethics at the entity level: culture and tone at the top, the law-vs-ethics distinction, anti-bribery regimes, social responsibility, and the fast-growing data-ethics topic.

Culture & codes

Tone at the topCorporate culture is set by leadership ("tone at the top"). A code of ethics/conduct formalizes values; SOX §406 requires disclosure of whether a public company has a code for senior financial officers. Core values often cited: transparency, sustainability, responsibility. Beware groupthink (consensus pressure suppresses dissent).
Legal vs ethical
  • Legal = rules-based (the minimum, enforceable).
  • Ethical = principles-based (higher bar; not all unethical acts are illegal).
Carroll's CSR pyramid
  • Economic → Legal → Ethical → Philanthropic (bottom to top).

FCPA vs UK Bribery Act

US FCPAUK Bribery Act
ScopeBribery of foreign officials only + accounting/internal-control provisionsPublic and private bribery; broader
Facilitation paymentsAllowed (grease payments for routine acts)Prohibited — no exception
Two prongsAnti-bribery + books-and-records/internal controlAlso creates "failure to prevent bribery" corporate offence
The facilitation-payment splitThe classic exam contrast: FCPA permits small facilitating ("grease") payments for routine governmental actions; the UK Bribery Act has no facilitation exception and also covers private-sector bribery. FCPA's accounting provisions require accurate books and adequate internal controls.

Sustainability & data ethics

Triple bottom line
  • Planet · People · Profit (the 3 spheres of sustainability).
Data-ethics pillars
  • Fairness · Privacy · Transparency · Ownership · Accountability.
Privacy toolkit & regimesTechniques: anonymization, differential privacy; watch for linkage attacks (re-identifying "anonymous" data by combining datasets). GDPR rights: access, correction, portability, erasure, plus consent & appeal. US sectoral laws: FTC Act, HIPAA (health), COPPA (children under 13), GLBA (financial), FCRA (credit).
